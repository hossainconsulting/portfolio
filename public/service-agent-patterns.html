The board presents itself as 200 opportunities: trades, cleaning, landscaping, clinics, salons, repair shops, agencies, consultants, 25 ideas each. Read column by column, that holds. Read row by row and the same skeleton repeats in nearly every one — missed-call capture, booking, reminders, review requests, invoice chase, handoff summaries, FAQ. Salons' waitlist filler is Trades' cancellation soft filler. Clinics' insurance document chase-up is Trades' warranty question triage.

The unit of reuse is the pattern, not the vertical. Build the triage-and-handoff engine once and eight columns become eight configurations, not eight builds.

01

The risk tiers decide the architecture

Every pattern here carries a tier, and the tier is not a severity label for its own sake — it dictates where the answer is allowed to come from. This is the single most load-bearing distinction in the document.

Green 6

A wrong answer costs a minute of someone's time. A reminder fires twice; an ETA is off by ten minutes.

Architectural ruleGeneration is fine. Ship it.

Amber 15

A wrong answer costs trust, or a booking. A misrouted enquiry, a bad summary, a review request sent to an angry customer.

Architectural ruleGeneration anchored on a retrieved fact, with a measured error rate.

Red 4

A wrong answer costs money, safety or a legal breach. Coverage statements, clinical urgency, pricing, payment claims, anything touching a health record.

Architectural ruleThe answer comes from a retrieved action, never the model. The model may only phrase what the action returned.

The red-tier rule is the generalised form of the constraint the Meridian build runs on: coverage answers come from Warranty_Entitlement__c.Is_Currently_Covered__c, a formula field the agent retrieves, not a judgement it generates. The same logic extends to any statement a customer could reasonably act on or hold you to. Under the Australian Consumer Law a claim about coverage, remedy, repair rights or price is a representation — an agent that improvises one is a compliance problem wearing a chat interface.

03

What the board doesn't cost in

The infographic's implicit promise is that the idea is the hard part. It isn't. Four costs sit between any cell on that grid and a working system — and the first of them is most of the build.

Integration surface

The agent is perhaps a tenth of the work. Auth, sync, retry, reconciliation and the sandbox you test it in are the rest. Grouped by what they touch, in the Australian market:

Data readiness

Pattern 22 needs to know the job finished and went well. Pattern 01 needs call logs joined to customer records. Pattern 19 needs a ledger the agent can read. Most small operators cannot answer these questions today, and no amount of model quality fixes an unqueryable "job complete" flag.

Volume economics

A three-van plumbing business misses perhaps fifteen calls a week. At ten minutes recovered each that is two and a half hours — real, but small. The economics concentrate where volume does. Meridian, at 12,400 cases a month across 35 seats, sits on the right side of that line. The solo tradie in the board's hero image does not.

"Owned, not rented"

The board's closing claim is positioning, not architecture. You own the orchestration logic, the prompts and the data. You rent the model API, the SMS gateway, the scheduling SaaS and the host. Worth saying plainly to any client who repeats the phrase back to you.

The measurement you skipped

Every pattern here implies a baseline you needed before you built. Meridian's is stark and typical: 71% CSAT, 9:40 average handle time, 28% repeat contact, and 0% self-service deflection. An improvement you cannot count is an improvement that gets switched off in month four.

04

The Australian compliance layer

Roughly a third of the board's cells carry an obligation the board never mentions. The exposure is not evenly spread — it clusters hard in the retention patterns and in the entire clinics column.

RegimeBitesThe practical constraint
Spam Act 2003 (Cth)21, 22, 24, 25Commercial electronic messages need consent, sender identification and a functional unsubscribe. Review requests, reactivation and upsell offers are commercial.
Spam Act — transactional07, 08, 10, 11, 18, 20Messages about an existing booking or debt are generally factual rather than commercial. Still collect consent at booking; the line moves the moment an offer is attached.
Do Not Call Register Act 2006outboundApplies where a pattern places marketing calls rather than sending messages.
Privacy Act 1988 — APPsallAPP 3 collection, APP 5 notification, APP 6 use and disclosure, APP 11 security. Conversation transcripts are personal information.
Privacy Act — health04, 06, 10, 15, 20Health service providers are covered regardless of turnover — the small-business exemption does not apply. State law stacks on top, e.g. the NSW Health Records and Information Privacy Act 2002.
Australian Consumer Law04, 05, 17, 19, 23Statements about coverage, remedy, repair rights or price are representations. A generated one is misleading-conduct risk.

Add to this a practice standard rather than a statute: disclose that the customer is talking to an AI, and make the escalation path visible in the first message. Not yet a general legal requirement in Australia, but it is where expectations are heading and it costs nothing.

Not legal advice

General Australian context for a real build. Confirm with someone qualified before any of these patterns ship to customers.

The map to Meridian

A single cell, done properly

The whole engagement is essentially pattern 19 plus 02, 16 and 17, built to a standard the board never describes: coverage as a computed fact, restricted picklists so an import can't invent a fourth SLA tier, and a first-response clock — 48, 24 and 8 business hours — running on Australia/Sydney business hours, Mon–Sat 8:00–18:00, so a VIP breach can't happen overnight while nobody is working. The board sells breadth. The value is depth.

The gaps are deliberate

Patterns 21–25 need a marketing consent model Meridian doesn't have; 18–20 need a billing integration outside the six-week scope. Naming the cells you chose not to build, and why, is what separates a scoped engagement from an unfinished one.

06

The pick-one test

When a client points at the grid and asks which cell to build first, four criteria, in this order.

  1. 01Frequency

    Does it happen daily? Weekly is too rare to learn from.

  2. 02Blast radius

    Green tier only for a first build. Never start on a red cell.

  3. 03Baseline

    Can you measure today's performance before you build? If not, you cannot prove the thing worked.

  4. 04Single integration

    One system to write to. Two systems triples the timeline.

Scored that way, the board's own "best places to start" box turns out to be right: missed-call follow-up, bookings and reminders, enquiry qualification, review follow-up. Patterns 01, 06, 07 and 21/22 — two green, two amber, all with an obvious baseline.

The Meridian corollary, and the rule the engagement is built on: define how you count a deflected conversation, build the report, then launch. Not the other way around.
